This session didn't start with an equation. It started with six words, lifted from a stranger's video about a social experiment — guess if she's single, from across the room, on appearance alone.

Why it mattered

Six words turned out to be a compressed restatement of ŷ − y_reality. Appearance is θᵗ. Her actual answer is the ground truth. The gap between them is the only thing worth measuring — and everything that followed in this session, sensitized neurons, diagnostic syntax, a 2016 linguistics column, was the same gap, relocated to a different substrate.

Three relocations

Folk psychology names it triggers. Spinal neurophysiology names it central sensitization. Generative syntax names it adjective order. None of the three vocabularies knew about the other two when they were built.

Finding 1 · Reading the phrase through θᵗ → ŷ → ε

Triggers are priors.
Ears are data.

θᵗ is the colloquial "trigger" — the accumulated weighted prior firing before any new input arrives. "Ears" is Eₓ(τ), the raw event a guess is supposed to integrate before it's allowed to become an output.

The phrase, formalized

── Trigger pathway ───────────────────────────────────────
θᵗ (trigger) → ŷ (the guess) · skips the integral

── Ears pathway ──────────────────────────────────────────
Eₓ(τ) (ears) → y_reality (her actual answer)

── What's left over ──────────────────────────────────────
ε = ŷ − y_reality

The social experiment is measuring ε in real time. Off-the-cuff guesses skip the integral and run L₀ straight to output — fluent, fast, ungrounded. λ_grounding ≈ 0.

Where it strains

Ears alone don't make θᵗ optional

L₀ is necessary infrastructure, not a contaminant. You can't initialize a forward pass without a prior. The fix isn't deleting triggers, it's gating their output behind a y_reality check before it reaches action.

Self-report is still a σ²

Even listening with your ears only swaps one unmodeled uncertainty for another. If y_reality here is her self-report, that's the same fragility as patient-stated goals in the clinical twin — people misreport themselves too. Relationship status just happens to be unusually verifiable.

One good listen isn't a backward pass

w_{t+1} = w_t − γ|ε|² describes an iterative update across many events. The line is an inference-time correction, not a training-time one — it fixes this guess, not the heuristic that produced it.

Acute vs. chronic

Acute

NMDA-mediated wind-up at the dorsal horn rewrites the tensor at the point of write, before any thalamic gate runs. By the time the signal reaches the gate, there's no false positive to catch — the input now genuinely carries nociceptive amplitude.

Chronic

Descending facilitation from RVM and amygdala — supraspinal, higher-order structures — actively maintains the sensitized state. The inhibitory pathway that should regularize instead facilitates. Chronic allodynia collapses into the same failure named elsewhere as regularization collapse, just instantiated in nociception.

Where / What / How

"Where" (touch, a benign modality) is correct. "How" (the threat-response pathway) executes exactly as instructed. Only "what" (the label) is wrong — and the system still produces total dysfunction, because the corrupted axis sits upstream of the other two and they trust it completely.

What the fix has to be

Graded exposure doesn't argue the patient out of noticing touch — they're already attending fully, which is exactly what hurts. The fix runs at the update-rule level: small γ, narrow correctly-labeled low-ε stimuli, walking the receptive field back down.

Mark Forsyth's rule for English adjective order, surfaced in the column: opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose, then noun. Native speakers apply it without ever being taught it, and violate it only by accident — get the order wrong and the sentence sounds, in Forsyth's phrase, like the work of a maniac. It's a rank-reduction sequence — the same grammar as opinion→...→purpose→noun, and the same grammar as Tensor→...→Scalar. Distance from the noun tracks how classifying a property is, not how independent it is. Three vocabularies converging on the same architecture without knowing about each other.

Hidden in plain sight

Culture | Johnson

Most people don’t know they know most of the grammar they know.

WHO can say what order should be used to list adjectives in English? Mark Forsyth, in “The Elements of Eloquence”, describes it as: opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose and then Noun. “So you can have a lovely little old rectangular green French silver whittling knife. But if you mess with that word order in the slightest you’ll sound like a maniac.” Mr Forsyth may have exaggerated how fixed adjective order is, but his little nugget is broadly true, and it has delighted people to examine something they didn’t know they knew.

Clearly, then, the discipline of linguistics needs a marketing overhaul, because this is exactly what linguistics consists of: describing the rules, many of them hidden and not obvious, of the human language ability. Given how eagerly word-nerds recently shared this tit-bit about adjective order on social media, the lecture-halls for linguistics classes should be crammed to the rafters.

Instead, as most linguists only too ruefully admit, upon confessing their profession at cocktail parties they tend to be told: “Oops, better watch my grammar around you.” Just as many psychologists moan that outsiders think the discipline is mainly about abnormal psychology, linguists haven’t sufficiently spread the word that they are not out to ban split infinitives or correct the misuse of “whom”. They consider themselves scientists (in a discipline that overlaps with psychology, cognitive science and others) in trying to learn how the human mind works.

They’ve found out many wonderful things about rules you know, but don’t know you know. For example, a question can be formed from a statement by turning the questioned element into a question-word (like “where”) and moving it to the front of a sentence. “Steve went to Toronto. Where did Steve go?” But that doesn’t work when the element in question is itself a clause: in “John wonders where Steve went to university” “went” can’t become “Where does John wonder that Steve went to university?” Everyone knows that the latter is awkward or even unacceptable, but very few people outside the world of linguistics know why. In fact, it took linguists themselves quite a while to work out the details.

There are hidden rules not just in grammar, but at every level of language production. Take pronunciation. The –s that marks a plural in English is pronounced differently depending on the previous consonants: if the consonant is “voiced” (ie, the vocal cords vibrate, as in “v”, “g” and “d”), then the –s is pronounced like a “z”. If the consonant is “unvoiced” (like “f”, “k” and “t”), then the –s is simply pronounced as an “s”. Every native English-speaker uses this rule every day. Children master it by three or four. But nobody is ever taught it, and almost nobody knows they know it.

Because linguists spend their careers trying to tease out what people actually do say and why, they get cross when people equate “grammar” with a host of rules that most people don’t actually observe. Take the so-called rule against ending sentences with a preposition. In fact, saying things like: “What are you talking about?” is deeply embedded in the grammar of English. “About what are you talking?” strikes real speakers of English as absurd. So it annoys linguists to no end to hear the latter “rule” associated with “grammar”, while the real, intricate grammar already embedded in the mind is ignored.

Sometimes our mental grammars don’t know what to do with unusual cases. Take the newish verb “to greenlight”, meaning to approve a project. What is its past tense? “Light” has the past tense “lit”. But some people go for “greenlighted” (Variety, a film-industry magazine, prefers this) whereas others go for “greenlit”. Why the confusion? It’s because “to greenlight” was formed anew from a noun phrase, “a green light”. One mental rule is that new words are always regular; hence “greenlighted”. But other people’s mental grammars see “greenlight” as a form of the verb “to light”, an existing irregular verb with the past tense “lit”; hence “greenlit”.

This implicit grammatical knowledge overwhelms, in its intricacy and depth, the relatively few rules that people must be consciously taught at school. But since the implicit stuff is hidden in plain sight, it gets overlooked. It is cheering to see that things like the adjective-order rule can go viral on social media. Perhaps it can make people more likely to associate “grammar” not with drudgery, but with fascinating self-discovery.

Closing · Session 003

The mathematics
was always there.

Three vocabularies, built for unrelated purposes, by unrelated disciplines, decades apart — folk psychology, spinal neurophysiology, generative syntax — all turned out to be reading the same coordinate.

ε

Every one of them is a version of the same question: what's the gap between what the prior predicted and what the input actually was — and who decided which one gets to be called real?

The clinical layer's open question — what y_reality should be for a patient trajectory — wasn't resolved this session either. But three more candidate vocabularies arrived to sharpen what an answer would need to look like: a self-reported relationship status, a von Frey filament reading, and a century and a half of descriptive linguistics. None of them transfers directly. All three confirm the same architecture is doing the work, whether or not it has a name yet.
